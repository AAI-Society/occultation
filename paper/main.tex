\vspace{-0.5em}
\noindent{\small
A desk study assessed two numbers against a \deskbudget{}
per-action budget for anonymous agent credentials --- BBS+ presentation at
about \deskpresentnaive{}, verification at about \deskverifynaive{}
--- and called each acceptable, because each is below \deskbudget{}
on its own. Summed, they are \desktotalnaive{}, which is over. With
cached pairing pre-computation the sum is \desktotalcached{}: under the
limit by \deskheadroomcached{}, a margin this paper's tool reports as
\texttt{MARGINAL} rather than a pass, because \deskheadroomcached{} is not
headroom for the application, the network, or writing the evidence record.
We built \occultation{}, a Rust tool that assesses presentation and
verification as one composed path rather than two separate ones, over real
BBS+ signatures on BLS12-381 and a real Ed25519 baseline. On our hardware the
absolute numbers are roughly an order of magnitude smaller than the desk
study's, and --- the finding the magnitude alone would hide --- the
\emph{shape} of the correction survives the hardware change while the shape
of an unrelated optimization does not: cached pairings save
\deskcachedsavingpct{} of verification in the desk study's figures and
\measuredcachedsavingpct{} of it in ours. The composition error is
arithmetic, not a property of any one machine. Pre-computation is what makes
the composed path fit at all, which converts a performance question into a
security one: what happens when the pre-computed pool runs out under burst?
We simulate it. The pool stalls; it does not reuse, and reuse is not
configurable, because we show precisely what reuse costs --- an observer of
two presentations sharing one blinding factor recovers the signature scalar
and every undisclosed attribute by subtraction, which our test suite performs
rather than merely asserts. Stalling is not free either: under a sustained
\poolburstratesi{} burst against a $\sim$\poolrefillratesi{} refill rate, the
worst response in a \poolwindow{} window is \poolamplification{}$\times$ an
idle one, a timing side channel with a duration-invariant signature ---
overload factor \pooloverloadfactor{}$\times$, growth per request
\poolgrowthperrequest{}$\times$ an idle response, opening at request
\poolonsetrequest{}. Separately, we measure the anonymity set the hardware
attestation layer actually has to work with: \anonfleetsize{} identical
hosts, after six weeks of ordinary operational drift in patch level and
microcode, partition into \anonconfigs{} distinguishable groups of
\anonsizes{} --- \anonsingletons{} host alone on its configuration before
any credential is even presented. Effective set size \anoneffectiveset{} of
\anonfleetsize{}; entropy \anonentropy{}. Two of the four layers this paper's
subject construction proposes --- hardware attestation and threshold escrow
--- are implemented here only as cost-representative stubs with no security
whatsoever, and every number that touches them says so in the sentence that
states it, not in a footnote.}

\section{The Problem}
\label{sec:intro}

The Proof-of-Control standard's identity domain, C5, has six requirements
--- the thinnest of its six domains --- and carries open issue
2~\cite{povocopenissues}: whether an autonomous agent's actions can be
independently verifiable and attributable when it matters, while being
unlinkable across relying parties the rest of the time. A working-group
member raised it in the plainest possible terms: enterprises need to deploy
agents without disclosing customer PII or corporate identity to third-party
vendors, and the best way to secure information is not to hand it out
unnecessarily.

The tension is structural, not incidental. Every other part of the standard
this domain sits inside pushes toward \emph{more} identifiable evidence: an
explicit \texttt{agent\ub id}, an \texttt{initiating\ub user}, a hash chain
that links every action to every action before it, anchors published where
anyone can see them. A hash-chained, publicly anchored log of an agent's
actions is close to the worst case for unlinkability: it is a permanent,
ordered, cryptographically bound dossier. Making that unlinkable is not a
matter of omitting a field from a record. It requires a credential layer
whose presentations do not link to each other, sitting underneath a log
layer built, by design, to chain everything together.

A desk study assembled a concrete four-layer answer --- hardware
attestation, an anonymous credential, an evidence log, and a threshold
escrow for accountability --- and estimated what it would cost, using
published benchmarks of other people's hardware rather than measuring
anything. The estimate contained an arithmetic error large enough to flip
its own headline conclusion, and finding that error is most of what this
paper is about. The desk study assessed presentation and verification
against the standard's \deskbudget{} per-action budget
\emph{separately}: presentation at about \deskpresentnaive{}, under
budget; verification at about \deskverifynaive{}, also under budget.
Both halves passing is not the same claim as the sum passing, and the sum
--- \desktotalnaive{} --- does not. No cryptographic assumption in the
desk study is wrong. The mistake is in the addition, and it survives being
pointed out only because presentation and verification happen on different
machines at different times, which makes it easy to grade them on separate
report cards.

This paper reports what happens when that composed path, and the two
security questions the desk study's own numbers raise but do not answer ---
whether the fix for the composition error is itself sound, and how large an
anonymity set the hardware layer actually leaves standing --- are measured
rather than estimated.

\section{What Others Have Built}
\label{sec:related}

The mechanisms this design needs are mature. Applying them to a
machine-speed, hash-chained, publicly anchored agent log is not, and every
citation in this section is doing double duty: establishing that the
primitive exists and is trusted, and marking the gap between that primitive
and this application.

\paragraph{Hardware attestation without a platform identifier.} Direct
Anonymous Attestation~\cite{daa} lets a TPM prove that \emph{some} valid TPM
produced a statement without revealing which one. It is adopted in TPM~2.0
and standardized as ISO/IEC~20008~\cite{iso20008}. The elliptic-curve
variant, ECDAA, is the practical form used in FIDO and is the closest
existing answer to the hardware layer this construction needs. It is also
the layer we do not implement: Section~\ref{sec:construction} explains why
it is modelled rather than built.

\paragraph{Anonymous credentials.} Camenisch--Lysyanskaya
credentials~\cite{cl2001}, Idemix~\cite{idemix}, and U-Prove~\cite{uprove}
are the lineage this paper's credential layer descends from. BBS+ signatures
over pairing-friendly curves~\cite{bbsplus,bls12381}, with a maturing
IETF/W3C track for selective disclosure~\cite{bbsdraft}, are the concrete
choice the desk study made and the one we implement over BLS12-381. Also
relevant, and less well known, is SPSEQ-UC~\cite{spseq}, structure-preserving
signatures on equivalence classes over updatable commitments: it targets
re-randomization directly, which is the property a log layer built from
these credentials would need and which our evidence-log layer does not yet
touch.

\paragraph{Group and ring signatures.} Chaum and van Heyst's group
signatures~\cite{chaumvanheyst} and Rivest, Shamir and Tauman's ring
signatures~\cite{ringsig} are the other route to ``some member of a group
signed this.'' Group signatures include an opening authority, which is the
shape our escrow layer generalizes to a quorum.

\paragraph{Unlinkable, bounded tokens.} Privacy Pass~\cite{privacypass} and
the VOPRF construction beneath it~\cite{voprf} are the closest deployed
analogue to ``unlinkable but rate-limited,'' and the desk study's own cost
table lists a Privacy Pass token at roughly \SI{850}{\micro\second}, sub-ms
amortized when pre-fetched in batches --- cheap enough that it is plausibly
the mechanism that makes an unlinkable design practical rather than merely
possible, though it is out of scope for the construction this paper
measures.

\paragraph{Threshold cryptography and escrow.} Threshold ElGamal, distributed
key generation, and Chaum--Pedersen DLEQ proofs for verifiable partial
decryption~\cite{chaumpedersen} are the machinery that turns ``escrow'' from
a standing backdoor into a quorum action with a public receipt. The
literature that keeps that machinery honest is the key-escrow critique in
\emph{Keys Under Doormats}~\cite{keysunderdoormats} and the specific,
documented failure modes of the Clipper Chip~\cite{clipperchip}: a
centralized depository, fragile operational control, and no transparency.
Section~\ref{sec:construction} returns to this literature when it explains
why our escrow stub's absence from the real half of this tool is not an
oversight.

\paragraph{Transport-layer linkability.} Oblivious HTTP~\cite{ohttp} and
relay-based padding schemes are easy to forget and fatal to omit: an
unlinkable credential presented over a directly attributable TCP connection
is not unlinkable. This construction, and this paper, do not touch the
transport layer at all; Section~\ref{sec:open} says so explicitly rather
than leaving it as a silent gap.

\paragraph{Where this paper sits.} None of the above measures what BBS+
costs at agent action rates, composed as a single present-and-verify path,
against a fixed real-time budget, on real hardware. That measurement --- and
the arithmetic error it corrects --- is this paper's contribution. The
security question the correction's own fix raises, whether a pre-computation
pool is sound under burst, appears nowhere in this literature either,
because in most of it pre-computation is a performance detail rather than
the thing standing between the design and its own latency budget.

The two rows that matter most for what follows are the first two. Every
figure in Section~\ref{sec:results} that is not explicitly labelled
\texttt{MODELLED} rests on real BLS12-381 pairing arithmetic and a real BBS+
implementation, timed on real hardware. Every figure that touches ECDAA or
threshold escrow is a cost estimate from a stub with no cryptographic
content, and this paper does not use either stub to support any claim about
security --- only, where unavoidable, about cost, and always labelled.

The escrow stub's absence from the real half of Table~\ref{tab:boundary} is
deliberate, not an oversight, for the reason Section~\ref{sec:related}'s
escrow literature makes plain: a $k$-of-$n$ threshold ElGamal tag with a NIZK
of correct encryption is exactly the kind of machinery \emph{Keys Under
Doormats}~\cite{keysunderdoormats} and the Clipper Chip's documented
failure~\cite{clipperchip} warn is easy to build and hard to build safely
--- the danger is in the governance surrounding a working implementation as
much as in the cryptography itself, and a stub built only to be
cost-representative cannot accidentally be mistaken for a governance
guarantee this paper has not evaluated. Building and asserting the security
of a real threshold-opening path is future work, not something a
cost-measurement tool should attempt as a side effect.

The evidence-log and transport layers in Table~\ref{tab:layers} are not
implemented at all. They are P05's open questions 1 and 2
(Section~\ref{sec:open}), and nothing in this paper's measurements bears on
them.

\section{Measuring the Composed Path}
\label{sec:method}

The desk study's error was structural: it judged two numbers against one
budget by comparing each number to the budget separately. \occultation{}'s
\texttt{bench --composed} command exists specifically to make that
structural mistake unavailable. It composes a presentation cost and a
verification cost into a single \texttt{Composed} value, sums them, and
judges the sum --- never a half --- against the \deskbudget{}
budget defined once, in \texttt{src/cost.rs}, as the single source of truth
every command reads. A verdict is one of three values, not two:
\texttt{Fits}, \texttt{Misses}, or --- the variant that earns its place ---
\texttt{Marginal}, for a composition that is under budget by less than
5\,\% of it. Without that third value, \desktotalcached{} against a
\deskbudget{} budget reports as a pass, which is exactly the
desk study's own conclusion about its cached-pairing figure and exactly the
conclusion this tool exists to correct.

\texttt{bench --composed} reports two profiles side by side, never merged.
The \emph{desk-study} profile reproduces the published figures verbatim,
tagged \texttt{PUBLISHED}, with their citation --- these numbers are
constants, not measured on this stack, and they are reported so that the
composition arithmetic is checkable by a third party without access to our
hardware. The \emph{measured} profile times the same operations, here, with
a seeded RNG and a median of many iterations, tagged \texttt{measured here}.
The two profiles are expected to disagree in magnitude, because they come
from different silicon, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports exactly how
much and in what shape they disagree.

One row in the measured profile carries a third tag, \texttt{ESTIMATED},
and it needs a precise statement of what it is not. Nothing in
\occultation{} measures a pooled presentation's \emph{online} phase directly
--- the work left to do once a blinding factor has already been pre-computed
and pulled from the pool. The pooled row in the measured composed-path table
is derived from a cheaper proxy, two hash-to-scalar operations, standing in
for the challenge hash and field-arithmetic responses a real online phase
would perform. \texttt{occultation pool}, which is the command that actually
exercises the pool under load (Section~\ref{sec:pool}), does not close this
gap either: its \texttt{--online} cost defaults to this identical estimate
when the operator does not supply one. So the estimate and the pool
simulation are not independent evidence for each other, and the row's own
note says the estimate is almost certainly too low --- it implies a 99\,\%
reduction in presentation cost where the desk study's own pooled figure
implies 80\,\%. We report it because omitting the row would leave the
measured profile's pooled composition entirely unstated, and a caveated
estimate is more honest than a silent gap. It is not a claim that we have
measured a pooled presentation, and no number in this paper should be read
as one.

\subsection{The composed path, corrected}
\label{sec:results-composed}

\input{../results/deterministic/composed-table.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:composed} is the desk study's own published figures,
composed as one path instead of two. The naive composition misses the
budget outright. Caching pairings brings it to \texttt{MARGINAL} rather than
a pass --- under the limit, but by a margin smaller than the jitter of an
uncontended context switch. Only the pre-computation pool, combined with
cached pairings, fits with room, which is the paper's headline correction:
pre-computation is not an optimization on top of a design that already
works. It is the reason the design fits its own budget at all.

Table~\ref{tab:primitives} is the same operations, measured here on this
host rather than read from a publication.

\input{../results/measured/primitives-table.tex}

The measured composed path (\texttt{results/measured/composed.txt}, not
reproduced as a table because wall-clock figures are not diffed by CI) is
\measureddivergencefactor{}$\times$ \measureddivergencedirection{} here than
the desk study's published figures imply, and the composed BBS+ path costs
\measuredratiotobaseline{}$\times$ an Ed25519 sign-and-verify round trip on
this host. The composition error itself is unaffected by any of this: it is
an error in arithmetic, not a property of any one machine, and it reproduces
identically wherever the desk study's published figures are summed.

What the magnitude alone would hide is that the divergence is not uniform.
Cached pairings are the one optimization measured on both sides of the
comparison: the desk study's published figures attribute
\deskcachedsavingpct{} of verification cost to caching the pairing
computation, and the same optimization saves \measuredcachedsavingpct{}
here. A uniformly faster host would preserve the desk study's ratio between
optimized and unoptimized verification; this host does not, so the measured
block does not corroborate the desk study's optimization structure, which we
report as a finding in its own right rather than as noise to be averaged
away.

\subsection{Pool exhaustion under burst}
\label{sec:results-pool}

Section~\ref{sec:pool} argues that the pre-computation pool's soundness,
not its speed, is the question the correction above actually raises. The
numbers behind that argument, from the shipped burst configuration
(capacity 64, refill \poolrefillcostsi{}, online \poolonlinecostsi{},
burst rate \poolburstratesi{}, seed 7 --- the same configuration
\texttt{tests/acceptance.rs}'s acceptance test~2 exercises) are: of
\poolrequests{} requests over a \poolwindow{} window, \poolserved{} were
served immediately and \poolstalled{} stalled. The worst response was
\poolamplification{}$\times$ an idle one. That figure is a peak over the
observation window and grows linearly with it, so we report it beside its
duration-invariant companions: an overload factor of
\pooloverloadfactor{}$\times$ arrivals over refill, a growth rate of
\poolgrowthperrequest{}$\times$ an idle response added to each successive
stall once the pool runs dry, and an onset at request \poolonsetrequest{}
--- how briefly a burst has to be sustained before the channel opens. Every
one of \pooldistinctblinding{} requests received a distinct blinding factor;
reuse events: \poolreuseevents{}.

\subsection{Anonymity under fleet drift}
\label{sec:results-anonymity}

\input{../results/deterministic/anonymity-table.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:anonymity} answers a question the desk study's cost analysis
does not ask at all: given perfect unlinkability at the credential layer,
how large is the anonymity set the hardware attestation layer actually
leaves standing? \texttt{examples/fleet-drifted.toml} encodes
\anonfleetsize{} hosts of identical hardware after six weeks of the kind of
operational drift every
real fleet accumulates --- a canary batch here, a delayed patch there, a
microcode revision that shipped to some racks and not others. A uniform
fleet with none of that drift (\texttt{examples/fleet-uniform.toml}) is one
anonymity set the size of the whole fleet, by construction, and serves only
as the tool's control case; Table~\ref{tab:anonymity} is the drifted one,
which is the realistic one.

\section{Is the Pre-Computation Pool Sound?}
\label{sec:pool}

Section~\ref{sec:results-composed} established that pre-computation is what
lets the composed path fit its budget at all, not an optional speed-up on
top of a design that already worked. That reframing relocates the risk. The
question is no longer whether BBS+ is fast enough; it is whether the pool
that makes it fast enough is sound. Nobody asked this question before the
desk study's arithmetic error made pre-computation load-bearing, because
until then it looked like an optimization, and optimizations are not
normally where a security review looks.

\paragraph{The two available behaviours.} A pool of pre-computed blinding
factors sitting in front of a burst of demand has exactly two ways to
respond once it is empty: it can make the caller wait for a fresh item, or
it can hand back one that has already been used. \occultation{}'s
\texttt{PrecomputationPool} does the first and never the second. On
exhaustion, \texttt{acquire} returns \texttt{None} rather than a used item,
and there is no configuration flag or code path under which it returns one.
Section~\ref{sec:results-pool} reports the cost of choosing to stall: under
a sustained burst, the delay does not plateau, it grows with the length of
the burst, and that growth is a timing side channel with a
duration-invariant signature --- an observer of an agent's response latency
alone can read off how overloaded it is and how long it has been that way.

\paragraph{Why the alternative is not a lesser evil.} Reusing one set of
blinding scalars across two presentations is not merely a weakening of
unlinkability; it discloses the credential outright, and this is provable
rather than asserted. A BBS+ presentation's proof of knowledge has the
Schnorr-style shape common to sigma protocols: for a hidden value $x$ (the
signature's blinding scalar, or an undisclosed attribute) with blinding
$\tilde{x}$ and Fiat--Shamir challenge $c$, the response the verifier sees
is
\begin{equation}
\hat{x} = \tilde{x} + c \cdot x \pmod{r}.
\end{equation}
If two presentations to two different relying parties reuse the same
blinding $\tilde{x}$ but necessarily bind to two different challenges $c_1
\neq c_2$ --- the challenge is derived from a per-session nonce, so
identical challenges would themselves require a coincidence --- then
subtracting the two responses cancels the blinding entirely:
\begin{equation}
\hat{x}_1 - \hat{x}_2 = (c_1 - c_2)\, x
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x = \frac{\hat{x}_1 - \hat{x}_2}{c_1 - c_2}.
\end{equation}
This is not a hypothetical weakness. \texttt{pool.rs}'s test
\texttt{reusing\ub one\ub set\ub of\ub scalars\ub leaks\ub the\ub signature\ub and\ub every\ub hidden\ub attribute}
performs exactly this division on the reused presentation's responses,
against a real signature and real hidden attributes, and asserts that the
recovered value equals the signature's scalar and every undisclosed
attribute in order. The two presentations are also linkable on sight before
any arithmetic is done, because they share the same commitment values
outright. Reuse is not a smaller version of the unlinkability property
failing; it is that property's negation, handed to whoever sees both
transcripts, in closed form.

\paragraph{What the type system buys, stated precisely.} \texttt{PoolItem}
is not \texttt{Clone} and its \texttt{consume} method takes \texttt{self} by
value, so \emph{accidental} reuse --- holding an item, copying it, or
re-acquiring it from the pool a second time --- is impossible: the fields
are private, the type cannot be duplicated, and \texttt{acquire} never
returns an item to its own queue. This is worth stating exactly because it
is easy to overstate. \emph{Deliberate} reuse is not prevented.
\texttt{consume} places no bound on what its closure returns, and the
scalars underneath are an ordinary \texttt{Clone}able type with public
fields, so \texttt{item.consume(|s| s.clone())} yields an owned copy that
outlives the item, and the lower-level proving function that accepts
pre-chosen scalars is itself public. What the design buys is not a guarantee
that reuse cannot happen; it is that any such reuse is one conspicuous,
greppable \texttt{.clone()} call at the point it happens, rather than an
emergent property of a pool running dry under load --- which is precisely
the failure mode this paper's measurement is about. A code reviewer looking
for that call will find it. A load test will not, because there is nothing
under load that produces it: exhaustion produces a stall, and only a stall.

\paragraph{What we are not claiming.} We have not built or evaluated a third
option beyond stall and reuse, and P05's own open question --- whether pool
refill activity, running as idle-time work inside the protected environment,
correlates with agent activity in a way an observable host resource (power
draw, cache behaviour, scheduling) could exploit --- is untouched by this
paper's measurements. \texttt{pool}'s simulation also treats refill as a
fluid approximation of a single worker rather than a discrete-event queue;
the approximation is stated in the code and does not change which of the two
behaviours is chosen, but it means the specific latency numbers in
Section~\ref{sec:results-pool} are a model of one refill strategy, not a
measurement of every plausible implementation.

\section{What We Still Don't Know}
\label{sec:open}

Four questions this tool does not answer, in the order the desk study's own
open-questions list raises them:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt,topsep=3pt]

\item \textbf{Can a log operator correlate re-randomized commitments by
posting time and size, even if the commitments themselves hide everything
cryptographically?} The construction's evidence-log layer proposes
per-session ephemeral chains with roots posted as re-randomizable Pedersen
commitments, specifically so that a log operator cannot reconstruct a global
action sequence from the commitments' contents. Nothing in this tool touches
that layer: it is not implemented, measured, or modelled here. Cryptographic
hiding of a value says nothing about the traffic pattern of when and how
often that value is posted, and an operator who can observe posting times
and sizes has a side channel that no property of the commitment scheme
addresses. This is the specific gap P05 names and the specific thing to
prove or refute, and this paper does neither.

\item \textbf{What does tamper-evidence mean once the global chain is
replaced by independent per-session chains?} A single global hash chain
proves that no action was deleted. Independent per-session chains, the
construction's own proposal for unlinkability, prove only that no action was
deleted \emph{from a session an auditor already knows exists}: an agent that
suppresses an entire session leaves no gap for anyone to notice. This may be
the more consequential result available from this whole research direction
--- that unlinkability and completeness-of-record are in tension rather than
independently achievable --- and this paper's measurements say nothing about
it either way, because the evidence-log layer is out of scope here.

\item \textbf{Revocation.} Revoking a compromised agent's credential without
linking its past actions, and without an accumulator update cost that scales
badly with fleet size, is historically the hardest part of deploying
anonymous credentials, and it is barely touched by the desk study this paper
corrects. \occultation{} has no revocation mechanism, measured or modelled.

\item \textbf{Is an anonymity set built from TCB configuration diversity
even the right set to be measuring?} Section~\ref{sec:results-anonymity}
answers ``how large is the set a real fleet's hardware diversity leaves
standing,'' which is a question worth answering on its own, but it is not
the same question as ``what is the anonymity set a colluding pair of relying
parties should be evaluated against.'' The right set might be agents within
one enterprise, enterprises sharing one credential issuer, or TEE hardware
generations across many unrelated fleets, and the answer changes who the
scheme protects against whom. Our calculator partitions whatever fleet
description it is given; it has no opinion about which fleet is the
relevant one, and supplying that opinion is future work, not something this
measurement resolves by itself.

\end{enumerate}

Two structural omissions sit above these four and are worth naming rather
than leaving implicit. The evidence-log and transport layers of
Table~\ref{tab:layers} are not implemented, measured, or modelled anywhere
in this artifact: the first is questions 1 and 2 above, and the second ---
Oblivious HTTP, relay padding, and everything that stops an unlinkable
credential from riding over a directly attributable connection --- is named
by P05 and untouched by this tool entirely. Every figure this paper reports
about cost, exhaustion, or anonymity should be read as a statement about the
credential and hardware-attestation layers alone.
